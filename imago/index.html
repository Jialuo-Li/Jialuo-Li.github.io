Imago:Maturing Visual Generation with Reinforcement Learning

  1. Overview
  2. §3 Evaluation
  3. §4 Reward
  4. §5 Corpus
  5. §6 Imago
  6. BibTeX

Modern text-to-image generators already possess vast generative capacity, yet under compositional pressure they omit entities, mis-bind attributes, and break spatial relations. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the natural mechanism to mature this capacity, but it has been studied mostly through optimization algorithms, leaving the reward signal and the prompt corpus underexplored. We first build an evaluation suite that does not saturate, then study both variables under one fixed RL setup, and combine the findings into Imago, a post-training recipe that turns FLUX.2-Klein-Base-4B and Z-Image into Imago-4B and Imago-6B.

What we find

  1. Long prompts keep the benchmarks informative. Across seven compositional benchmarks with prompts from about 9 to 539 words, the mean score of 19 models falls from 0.70–0.77 on short prompts to 0.55–0.62 on long ones. PickScore, the preference model least correlated with the others, measures visual appeal. Evaluation ↓
  2. A small MLLM asked for a score is the best reward. Zero-shot PromptScore, a 1–10 grade from Qwen3.5-9B, tracks all seven benchmarks (Pearson r = 0.96–0.99) and trains the best models without the reward hacking of preference-trained rewards. Larger reward backbones do not help. Reward ↓
  3. The prompt corpus shapes what RL teaches. Prompts that balance entities, attributes, and relations give the strongest overall gains. Short and long prompts help in complementary ways; mixing them uniformly does not reliably combine the two, while training on short prompts first and long prompts second does. Prompt corpus ↓
  4. Imago leads its size class and rivals far larger models. Imago-4B and Imago-6B score highest in their size classes on all five compositional benchmarks while raising PickScore, and Imago-6B surpasses the 32B FLUX.2-dev on medium and long prompts. Imago ↓

Designing the reward signal and the training environment is as essential to effective reinforcement learning as refining the optimization algorithm.

§3How should RL recipes be evaluated?

Evaluating compositional capability and visual appeal

We evaluate models along two dimensions: compositional capability, how faithfully an image follows its prompt, and visual appeal, its overall perceptual quality. Widely used compositional benchmarks such as GenEval and DPG-Bench rely on short prompts like “a red dog” and have begun to saturate on modern models.

Seven benchmarks across three prompt lengths

We select seven benchmarks and group them by mean prompt length: short (under 100 words), medium (100 to 300 words), and long (over 300 words), with Kimi-K2.6 as the MLLM judge throughout. Evaluating 19 text-to-image models shows that the suite avoids saturation: mean normalized scores range from 0.70 to 0.77 on short-prompt benchmarks and from 0.55 to 0.62 on long-prompt ones ().

A visual-appeal evaluator independent of the others

Learned preference models are the usual measure of visual appeal. If the evaluator correlates with the model used as the RL reward, however, it favors models tuned on that same objective and can mask reward hacking. We score the outputs of the 19 models on all seven benchmarks with four preference models and correlate them pairwise over the pooled samples. PickScore is the least correlated with the others (r̄ = 0.51, against 0.62–0.69), so we use it to measure visual appeal, on DrawBench prompts whose concise descriptions modern models reliably satisfy.

§4Which reward should guide RL?

A zero-shot PromptScore outperforms preference-trained rewards

A reward must first track the target, and a good evaluator is not necessarily a good training signal. We therefore examine candidate rewards from both sides: as evaluators, by how closely they track the compositional benchmarks, and as RL rewards, by the models they train.

4.1As an evaluator: does it track compositional capability?

For each benchmark, we correlate a reward's mean score with the benchmark score across the 19 models. We compare four preference-trained rewards (HPSv3, PickScore, ImageReward, and UnifiedReward) with two zero-shot ones: CLIPScore and PromptScore, which asks Qwen3.5-9B to grade prompt–image alignment from 1 to 10. UnifiedReward shares PromptScore's Qwen3.5-9B backbone and differs mainly in preference tuning, so this pair isolates the effect of that tuning.

PromptScore prompt template
SystemYou are an expert image evaluator. Please assess how accurately the provided image matches the text prompt. Apply maximum strictness. Score the alignment on a scale from 1 to 10, where 1 means completely mismatched and 10 means perfectly matched. Output your response strictly in the following JSON format: {"score": <integer_between_1_and_10>}
User<image> Text Prompt: "<prompt>"

Preference tuning costs alignment: UnifiedReward trails PromptScore on every benchmark despite sharing its backbone, which suggests that preference fine-tuning biases a model toward broad perceptual preferences.

To find what makes PromptScore work, we ablate one component at a time: a binary criterion that credits only a complete match instead of the 1–10 grade, and Gemma-4-E4B in place of Qwen3.5-9B (). Binary scoring loses correlation as prompts lengthen, since a single missed detail turns a partial match into a failure. Changing the backbone leaves the correlations nearly unchanged, so the graded format, not the choice of MLLM, drives them.

4.2As a training signal: does a better evaluator train a better model?

We next use four of these rewards for RL and hold everything else fixed: DiffusionNFT fine-tunes FLUX.2-Klein-Base-4B for 500 steps and Z-Image for 300 steps, with neither classifier-free guidance (CFG) nor KL regularization during training. PromptScore trains the best model on all seven benchmarks for both base models (), mirroring its correlations: the reward that tracks the benchmarks better also trains the better generator.

PromptScore also keeps visual appeal on par with the preference-trained rewards. Those rewards are prone to reward hacking instead: HPSv3, for example, adds subjects the prompt never asked for to raise its aesthetic score (). We attribute part of PromptScore's quality gain to training without CFG: because it scores unguided samples, the generator must produce images clear enough for the MLLM to recognize what they depict.

Explicit scoring versus prompt likelihood. SpectraReward is another training-free reward from a frozen MLLM; it measures how likely the MLLM is to reconstruct the prompt from the image. For an image x, a prompt c = (c1, …, cL), and the evaluation template q, the two rewards are

r_PS(x, c) = s / 10; r_SR(x, c) = (1/L) Σ log p(c_l | x, c_<l)

With the same frozen Qwen3.5-9B, PromptScore trails SpectraReward slightly on short prompts but leads on long ones for both base models (). Averaged over hundreds of tokens, an omitted entity or a wrong relation changes only a small part of the likelihood, whereas an explicit score penalizes it regardless of prompt length.

4.3Does a larger reward model help?

Under the same settings, we vary PromptScore's backbone: Qwen3.5 at 4B, 9B, and 27B, plus Gemma-4-31B and GLM-5.3-Flash (320B total, 18B active). Every backbone improves both base models substantially, but larger ones do not help consistently. Gemma-4-31B gives the strongest overall results, the 320B GLM-5.3-Flash performs on par with or below it, and within Qwen3.5, scaling from 4B to 27B yields no consistent improvement (). Benchmark correlation does not grow with size either: all five backbones reach a mean r between 0.972 and 0.987, and the 4B backbone already reaches 0.986.

Offline correlation predicts optimization efficacy. Benchmark correlation reliably forecasts downstream training outcomes. Evaluating this alignment directly identifies effective rewards, eliminating the cost of exploratory training sweeps across candidate signals.

Small pretrained MLLMs can provide robust zero-shot rewards. Graded scoring on a small frozen MLLM improves composition across prompt lengths without compromising visual appeal, avoiding preference reward hacking and the token dilution inherent to prompt likelihood.

§5What prompts should RL train on?

The prompt corpus is the RL environment

In RL for text-to-image generation, the prompt corpus defines what the generator explores, yet existing pipelines draw prompts from heterogeneous, uncontrolled pools. We construct controlled corpora that vary in prompt design and prompt length, and isolate how each affects the trained model.

5.1Building controlled prompt environments

Following scene-graph representations, we decompose prompts into entities (objects present), attributes (verifiable visual properties), and relations (spatial, physical, or structural interactions). We leave out holistic modifiers such as style and mood, which lack grounded visual referents, so that failures stem from composition rather than stylistic ambiguity. The corpus is built in two phases: we curate short prompts into Imago-Short, then expand each one into four longer prompts with Qwen3.5-27B, each following a different design.

5.2Effects of prompt design and prompt length

We fine-tune both base models on each design with graded PromptScore and the RL setup of §4. The balanced design delivers the most consistent gains on both base models (). We attribute this to its symmetry: spreading detail evenly across entities, attributes, and relations encourages the model to acquire these skills jointly rather than favoring one axis. We adopt the balanced corpus as Imago-Long.

Prompt length matters as well. Trained on Imago-Short, the models are stronger on concise benchmarks that emphasize direct attribute binding, such as TIIF-Bench; trained on Imago-Long, they excel on the detailed prompts of LongBench-T2I. Training on a single length regime cannot improve benchmarks of all lengths uniformly.

5.3Sequential training: from short binding to long composition

To let one model inherit the strengths of both corpora, we compare two schedules with the same number of training steps. Joint training samples uniformly from both corpora throughout; sequential training uses Imago-Short for the first half of the steps and then switches to Imago-Long. Joint mixing does not reliably match the better single-corpus run on each benchmark. Sequential training achieves the highest LongBench-T2I score on both base models, surpassing training on Imago-Long alone, while remaining competitive on short prompts ().

We attribute this to conflicting demands across prompt lengths. Short prompts require accurate local attribute binding, whereas detailed prompts require coordinating many objects and their relations; optimizing for both at once pulls the model in incompatible directions. Training on Imago-Short first establishes local binding, on which the Imago-Long phase then builds to compose dense, multi-relation scenes.

The prompt corpus defines the RL environment for text-to-image generation. Among prompt designs, balancing entities, attributes, and relations yields the strongest overall performance. Across prompt lengths, a short-to-long sequential schedule avoids the optimization conflicts of joint mixing, maintaining performance on concise prompts while unlocking long-prompt composition beyond dedicated long-prompt training.

§6What does the combined recipe achieve?

Imago: an online RL recipe for compositional generation

Imago combines these findings without modifying the generator's architecture. Graded PromptScore from Qwen3.5-9B is the only reward; prompts follow the sequential schedule from Imago-Short to Imago-Long; and DiffusionNFT optimizes LoRA adapters without CFG or KL regularization. Applied to FLUX.2-Klein-Base-4B and Z-Image, it produces Imago-4B and Imago-6B.

We compare Imago with 19 competitive models: proprietary systems, unified multimodal models, and diffusion and flow-matching models (). Imago-4B and Imago-6B achieve the highest scores across all five compositional benchmarks within their size classes, and both raise PickScore over their base models. Z-Image-Turbo, an RL-fine-tuned variant of Z-Image, also raises PickScore but drops on every compositional benchmark, showing that preference optimization can compromise prompt adherence, whereas Imago improves composition and visual quality together.

Beyond its size class, Imago-6B achieves the strongest medium- and long-prompt performance among open-weight models, outperforming the far larger FLUX.2-dev (32B) and rivaling the proprietary Nano-Banana. It also achieves the best SpatialGenEval score of all models. A structured RL post-training recipe can thus deliver compositional gains that previously required scaling model parameters.
