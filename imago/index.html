Recent text-to-image (T2I) models generate sharp, plausible imagery yet still fail to bind attributes, resolve spatial relations, or coordinate multi-object scenes as prompt descriptions grow denser. While online reinforcement learning (RL) has become central to post-training language models, visual RL recipes remain fragmented across coupled choices of reward models, prompt sets, and optimization schedules.

In Imago, we decouple and study the core components that govern online T2I RL—reward design and prompt corpus construction—under a single controlled testbed: online DiffusionNFT without classifier-free guidance or explicit KL regularization across two distinct flow-matching backbones (FLUX.2-Klein-Base-4B and Z-Image, 6B). Combining our findings yields Imago-4B and Imago-6B, demonstrating that principled RL post-training substantially matures compact visual generators.

§3 · Evaluation & Testbed

Measuring Compositional Adherence & Visual Appeal

Evaluating RL post-training requires separating two distinct axes: compositional prompt adherence across diverse prompt lengths and perceptual visual appeal. If an evaluator conflates aesthetics with alignment—or saturates on short captions—it masks reward hacking.

To select an informative visual appeal metric, we evaluate 19 baseline T2I models across four human-preference scorers: PickScore, ImageReward, HPSv3, and UnifiedReward. Because PickScore exhibits the lowest off-diagonal redundancy (r̄ = 0.51 vs. 0.62–0.69), we pair PickScore on DrawBench with Qwen3.5-27B judging across seven compositional benchmarks spanning three length regimes: Short (<100w), Medium (100–300w), and Long (>300w).

§4 · Reward Design

What Makes an Effective Reward Signal for T2I RL?

We compare classical alignment metrics (CLIPScore), human-preference models (HPSv3, UnifiedReward), generative prompt-reconstruction likelihood (SpectraReward), and zero-shot MLLM discriminative scoring across both benchmark-level correlation (19 baseline T2I models) and controlled RL optimization (500 steps on FLUX.2-Klein-Base-4B and 300 steps on Z-Image). Notably, UnifiedReward shares the exact Qwen3.5-9B backbone as our zero-shot reward, directly isolating the effect of human-preference fine-tuning.

For our primary discriminative reward, PromptScore, a frozen Qwen3.5-9B model scores image–prompt alignment on an integer rubric s ∈ {1, …, 10}; for generative scoring, SpectraReward averages log-likelihood across the L prompt tokens conditioned on the image:

rPS(x, c) = s / 10 ∈ [0.1, 1.0],      rSR(x, c) = (1 / L) ∑l=1..L log pθ(cl | x, c<l)

§4.2  Discriminative Rubric vs. Generative Prompt Reconstruction

Comparing standalone PromptScore and SpectraReward with the same frozen Qwen3.5-9B backbone reveals complementary length behavior: token-level prompt log-likelihood slightly leads on short prompts where nearly every word denotes a concrete object or attribute, whereas explicit rubric judgment scales better to long prompts where averaging across hundreds of tokens dilutes individual entity or relation omissions.

§4.3  Scaling the Reward Backbone (4B → 320B MoE)

Does a larger MLLM automatically yield a stronger RL reward? Keeping the PromptScore rubric and training budget fixed, we scale the frozen reward model across five backbones spanning two orders of magnitude: Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.5-27B, Gemma-4-31B, and GLM-5.3-Flash-320B-A18B. Larger backbones do not consistently produce stronger policies—even Qwen3.5-4B already reaches r̄ = 0.986, and scaling to 320B yields no systematic downstream advantage over 9B or 31B.

Takeaway 2 — Compact 4B–9B MLLMs Suffice as Compositional Rewards: Verifying explicit visual alignment is far less capacity-intensive than open-ended multimodal reasoning. A frozen Qwen3.5-9B server captures the full optimization signal while avoiding multi-GPU 27B–320B inference overhead.
§5 · Prompt Corpus & Schedule

Structuring & Scheduling Training Prompts

Even with a reliable reward, RL can only refine compositional behaviors that its training prompts exercise. Starting from ~3M Fine-T2I captions, we deduplicate embeddings with Qwen3-Embedding and filter ungrounded descriptions with Qwen3.5-27B to curate two 150K-prompt corpora:

  • Imago-Short (150K, ~30 words): Concise, visually grounded descriptions targeting crisp local attribute binding, spatial positioning, numeracy, and rendered text.
  • Imago-Long (150K, ~200 words): Multi-sentence scene specifications expanded via Qwen3.5-27B to balance all three objective scene-graph primitives—entities (distinct objects), attributes (intrinsic visual properties), and relations (spatial & action interactions)—without stylistic filler.

To test whether scene-graph balance matters independently of word count, we expand identical seed scenes into four length-matched (~200-word) corpora: Balanced, Entity-Centric, Attribute-Centric, and Relation-Centric.

§5.3–5.4  Prompt Length Regimes & Sequential Short → Long Scheduling

Training solely on Imago-Short sharpens local binding on short prompts (+5.0% on CompBench for FLUX.2-4B) but leaves multi-sentence composition behind (+8.8% vs. +14.3% on LongBench). Conversely, training solely on Imago-Long strengthens paragraph-level coordination while under-optimizing short-prompt precision.

Under an identical compute budget, sequentially transitioning from Imago-Short to Imago-Long with a midpoint optimizer/EMA reset consistently outperforms uniform joint mixing (Imago-Short ∪ Long) and reverse Long → Short ordering: Stage 1 establishes reliable local binding before Stage 2 scales global multi-entity composition.

Takeaway 3 — Curriculum Ordering Resolves Length Specialization: Balanced scene-graph structure maximizes transfer per word, and staging Short → Long preserves crisp local binding while scaling global multi-object composition—achieving the highest LongBench-T2I scores across both backbones.
§6 · Imago Models & Results

Unifying Reward, Corpus & Schedule into Imago-4B & Imago-6B

Combining our findings—online DiffusionNFT without CFG or explicit KL penalty, high-rank LoRA (r = 256), zero-shot PromptScore (Qwen3.5-9B), and Stage 1 Imago-Short → Stage 2 Imago-Long—produces Imago-4B (from FLUX.2-Klein-Base-4B, 500 + 500 steps) and Imago-6B (from Z-Image, 300 + 300 steps) on a single 8 × NVIDIA B200 node.
